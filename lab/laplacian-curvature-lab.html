Curvature is what the Laplacian sees beyond flat space

On surfaces, on triangle meshes and on graphs, every standard curvature can be read off Δ: from its mean values, its heat flow, its Bochner identity or its spectrum. Each section below measures one of them live, checks it against the theory, and hands the same computation to a Wolfram kernel.

Gauss and mean curvature, coloured on six surfaces

Gauss curvature is the flat Laplacian of the log conformal factor, \(K=-e^{-2u}\Delta_0u\), and the Laplace–Beltrami operator of the position vector is the mean-curvature vector, \(\Delta_gX=-2H\mathbf n\). Here both are computed from the parametrisation by finite differences and painted on the surface: blue negative, near-white zero, orange positive.

Gauss–Bonnet: \(\iint K\,dA=2\pi\chi\) on closed surfaces, so the sphere gives \(4\pi\) and the torus \(0\). Positive outside the torus, negative inside, zero on the top and bottom circles.

Ricci flow is a heat equation for the conformal factor

With \(g=e^{2u}(dx^2+dy^2)\), Ricci flow \(\partial_tg=-2\,\mathrm{Ric}\) becomes \(\partial_tu=e^{-2u}\Delta_0u=-K\). Start from a bumpy metric on the flat torus and watch the flow iron the curvature out. The total curvature stays exactly zero, as Gauss–Bonnet demands for \(\chi=0\).

The mean-value origin of Δ, on a curved surface

Laplacian Lab Section 1 derives Δ from sphere means in flat space. On a surface of constant curvature K the geodesic-circle mean picks up curvature at order r⁴:

\[M_rf=f+\frac{r^2}{4}\Delta f+r^4\Big(\frac{\Delta^2f}{64}+\frac{K\,\Delta f}{96}\Big)+r^6\Big(\frac{\Delta^3f}{2304}+\frac{K\Delta^2f}{1152}+\frac{K^2\Delta f}{1440}\Big)+O(r^8)\]

Errors of three approximations to the exact circle mean of \(f=e^x(1+z)^2+yz^3\) on the unit sphere (at the pole \(f=4,\ \Delta f=-4,\ \Delta^2f=-20,\ \Delta^3f=500\)), on log–log axes.

Error slopes

The flat formula is wrong at order r⁴ (slope 4). The K term fixes it (slope 6); the r⁶ terms push the error to slope 8.

Curvature is the first correction to Weyl’s law

The heat trace \(\operatorname{Tr}e^{t\Delta}=\sum e^{-\lambda_kt}\sim(4\pi t)^{-n/2}\big(\mathrm{Vol}+\tfrac t6\int S+\dots\big)\) (McKean–Singer). Two drums with the same area 4π: the unit sphere (K = 1) and a flat square torus (K = 0). Their spectra differ by exactly the curvature term.

At time t

Sphere eigenvalues l(l+1) with multiplicity 2l+1; the ratio to Weyl’s Area/(4πt) rises like 1 + t/3, slope \(\int S/(6\,\mathrm{Area})=1/3\). The flat torus stays at 1.

Bochner’s formula, and what a curvature bound controls

\(\tfrac12\Delta|\nabla f|^2=|\mathrm{Hess}f|^2+\langle\nabla f,\nabla\Delta f\rangle+\mathrm{Ric}(\nabla f,\nabla f)\). Integrated, it gives Lichnerowicz \(\lambda_1\ge nk\); along geodesics it gives the Laplacian comparison \(\Delta r\le(n-1)\sqrt k\cot(\sqrt k r)\) and Bishop–Gromov volume comparison. Its Γ₂ form, \(\Gamma_2\ge K\Gamma\), is the definition graphs copy. In Bakry–Émery’s Γ-calculus the gradient term is the carré du champ (“square of the field”) \(\Gamma(f)=\tfrac12(\Delta(f^2)-2f\Delta f)=|\nabla f|^2\), and \(\Gamma_2(f)=\tfrac12\Delta\Gamma(f)-\Gamma(f,\Delta f)\); both need only Δ, so they make sense on graphs.

Readings

Notebook §6: Bochner holds symbolically for an arbitrary f on S² and on the torus (variable K = cos v/(2 + cos v)); λ₁(S²) = 2 = nk, the equality case.

On a mesh, angle defect is curvature and the cotangent Laplacian is −2Hn

\((\Delta f)_i=\frac1{2A_i}\sum_j(\cot\alpha_{ij}+\cot\beta_{ij})(f_j-f_i)\), where \(\alpha_{ij},\beta_{ij}\) are the angles opposite edge \(ij\) and \(A_i\) is the vertex area. The angle defect \(\delta_i=2\pi-\sum\theta_i\) is how far the corner angles \(\theta_i\) of the triangles meeting at vertex \(i\) fall short of the flat \(2\pi\) (a cube corner: \(2\pi-3\cdot\tfrac\pi2=\tfrac\pi2\)); \(K_i\approx\delta_i/A_i\). The defects of a closed mesh add to exactly \(2\pi\chi\) (χ = V − E + F, the Euler characteristic), a discrete Gauss–Bonnet theorem that holds at every resolution.

Mean-curvature flow: a curve rounds itself off

\(\partial_tX=\Delta X=\kappa N\). In the plane this is curve shortening: any embedded curve becomes convex, then round, and shrinks to a point (Gage–Hamilton–Grayson). The curve is a polygon and \(\partial_s^2X\) is the graph Laplacian of the cycle \(C_n\), weighted by arc length. Time steps shrink with the edges (each step is at most 0.2 × the shortest edge squared, the stability limit of an explicit heat step), and the polygon is resampled by arc length, with fewer vertices as it shrinks, only when its edges grow uneven, so it stays smooth to the end. The enclosed area falls at exactly 2π per unit time because \(\oint\kappa\,ds=2\pi\).

Comparing two ball averages measures Ricci curvature

Ollivier’s curvature \(\kappa(x,y)=1-W_1(\mu_x,\mu_y)/d(x,y)\) with \(\mu\) the uniform measure on ε-balls satisfies \(\kappa\approx\varepsilon^2\,\mathrm{Ric}(v,v)/(2(n+2))=\varepsilon^2K/8\) on a surface. Sliding the surface along the geodesic carries one ball onto the other; a point at distance λ from the geodesic moves \(\delta\cos(\sqrt K\lambda)\), so κ is a plain integral over the ball.

Readings

Notebook 07-Ollivier §8 (and LaplacianCurvature §10.4): κ/ε² = 0.12496 (sphere), 0 (plane), −0.12504 (hyperbolic plane) at ε = 0.05.

This is the same comparison that defines Lin–Lu–Yau curvature on a graph, with neighbourhoods in place of balls.
